How it works

How SPARC works out what a night of charging costs, how much it saves, and how the simulation replays real nights. All it takes is adding up.

1. The price of one kWh

Price of a kWh= ComEd's price for that hour+ Delivery charge for that hour
  • ComEd's price changes every hour. ComEd posts a price every 5 minutes, and you pay the average of the hour's twelve prices. At night it is usually 2 to 5 cents per kWh, but it can spike, and it can even go below zero.
  • The delivery charge depends on which ComEd delivery plan you are on:
Standard delivery: the same every hour
Time-of-Day delivery: cheap at night, expensive from 1 to 7 p.m.

A real bill also has a fixed monthly charge, and per-kWh charges that are the same at every hour, such as taxes. They don't change when it is best to charge, so SPARC leaves them out. The costs on these pages are the part of the bill that the timing of charging can change.

5. How the simulation works

  1. Pick a night and a driver. The car plugs in at 8 p.m. and has to be charged by 7 a.m.
  2. Cut the night into 5-minute steps, 132 of them.
  3. At each step, the strategy decides whether to charge at full power or wait. It may only use what was known at that moment: the day-ahead prices, which come out around 4:30 p.m. the day before (the simulation waits until 5 p.m. to be safe), and the 5-minute prices posted so far, each about 7 minutes after it starts.
  4. SPARC's plan ranks the night's hours by day-ahead price plus delivery charge, and fills the cheapest hours first. It aims to finish 30 minutes early, and it stays out of summer weekday afternoons, when using power raises next year's capacity charge.
  5. Bill each kWh at its hour's final price plus delivery. No strategy sees the final price ahead of time.
  6. Check the car is charged by 7 a.m. In these tests it always was.
  7. Repeat for every night and all three drivers. A night with missing prices is skipped for every strategy alike, so the comparison stays fair.

6. What we compare

The default setup
Standard delivery, charging as soon as the car is plugged in. Most drivers start here, and every saving is measured against it.
SPARC's plan
Time-of-Day delivery, charging in the hours the day-ahead prices say are cheapest.
Perfect hindsight
Knows every real price in advance. Impossible in real life; it shows the most anyone could save on a night.
Battery buffer
Buys more on cheap nights and less on expensive ones, keeping the battery between 20% and 90% full. Tested with scripts, not in the app yet.
AI forecasts
SPARC's planner using an AI model's price forecast instead of the day-ahead price. Try them on the Test models page.

7. When is a difference real?

Two strategies are compared night by night, on the same nights. Some nights one wins, some nights the other. If the average gap is smaller than about two "standard errors" (a measure of how much the nightly gaps jump around), we call it a tie, because the gap could be luck. That is why some small differences are reported as "about the same".

Words used on these pages

kWh (kilowatt-hour)
A unit of energy. A Cybertruck's battery holds 123 kWh; driving 37 miles uses about 15.
¢/kWh
Cents per kilowatt-hour: the price of one kWh.
Day-ahead price
The price for each hour of tomorrow, set by the PJM market the afternoon before. A good forecast, but not what you pay.
Real-time price
What each hour actually costs: the average of its twelve 5-minute prices. This is what ComEd Hourly Pricing bills.
Delivery charge
What ComEd charges, per kWh, to bring the power to your home.
Time-of-Day delivery
A ComEd delivery plan that is cheap at night and expensive from 1 to 7 p.m.
Capacity charge
Part of next year's bill, set by how much power you use in the grid's busiest summer afternoon hours.
RMS error
The typical size of a forecast's miss, where big misses count extra. See Accuracy.
