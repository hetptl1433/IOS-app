What the studies found

  1. The day-ahead plan is hard to beat within a night. It charges in the cheapest hours by ComEd's published day-ahead prices and gets — of what perfect hindsight saves for the Average driver. Timers, price caps, live 5-minute reaction and learned forecasts, tuned on earlier nights and scored on 2026, all land within two standard errors of it.
  2. Better forecasts don't make better plans. Every trained and pretrained forecast has a lower RMS error than the day-ahead price, and every one saves less when the planner uses it.
  3. The battery is the lever. Buying more on cheap nights, topping up when a live price dips and skipping planned hours when one spikes adds — a year over the plan for the Average driver. It holds in every year, including the ones the rules were not tuned on.
  4. Plug-in time matters most. The plan saves — a year over charging on plug-in when plugged in at 8 p.m., but only — at 11 p.m.

The rule

A 123 kWh battery driven a normal distance doesn't need to refill every night. The buffer buys more on cheap nights and less on dear ones, using only what was known at the time.

  1. Fill up on cheap nights. At plug-in, rank tonight's cheap-hours price against the last 30 nights. The cheaper tonight is, the closer the plan fills towards the 90% cap; the day-ahead planner still picks the hours.
  2. Top up when a live price comes in low. Every 5 minutes, estimate the current hour's price from its posted 5-minute prices. Below the 20th percentile of recent nights, charge now.
  3. Skip a planned hour whose live price spikes above the 80th percentile, as long as tomorrow still stays above the floor.

The battery is never planned below 20% after a day's drive or above 90% after charging, and the floor held on every night. The settings were chosen on April–December 2025 nights, so 2024 and 2026 are out of sample.

How the backtests work

  • Prices as they were known. A strategy deciding at a given moment sees the day-ahead prices published the afternoon before, each 5-minute real-time price about 7 minutes after its interval starts, and weather forecasts issued by then. It never sees the settled hourly price it is billed at.
  • Billing follows ComEd. Each kWh pays its hour's settled price (the average of twelve 5-minute prices) plus that hour's delivery rate: standard, or Time-of-Day.
  • Physical limits. Charging power per 5-minute slot, a 30-minute finish-early margin and 90% charging efficiency. Summer weekday afternoons are blocked for planned strategies, because energy in peak hours sets next year's capacity charge.
  • Fair comparisons. Every strategy runs on the same nights. Ties mean within two standard errors of the paired night-by-night difference. Tuned rules are chosen on earlier nights and scored on later ones. Battery-buffer errors come from resampling whole months, because each night depends on the one before.
  • Checks on the data. ComEd's own published hourly prices were compared with the 5-minute feed, and a second environment fetched the prices directly from ComEd and reproduced the backtest exactly.

Limits

  • A backtest on 2024–2026 Chicago prices. Future prices, especially extremes like the January 2026 storm, will differ.
  • Driving is the same every day and the car is plugged in every night. Real drivers vary.
  • No strategy has run on a real car yet. Battery wear, charger quirks and the home's other load are not modelled.
  • Transmission and capacity charges on the rest of the bill are outside these comparisons, apart from avoiding capacity-risk hours.
